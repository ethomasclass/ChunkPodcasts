### SFX S1: howling blizzard wind building, a wooden door rattling then slamming open
### MUSIC M7 (blizzard tension) in, low

**VO 1: Cold open**
> [serious] Nebraska. January twelfth, 1888.
> The morning is strangely warm for winter. Kids walk to school in light jackets. Some don't bother with coats at all.
> In a small sod schoolhouse, a young teacher named Minnie Freeman is teaching class when the sky to the northwest turns a strange, bruised gray.
> [serious] Then it hits. A wall of wind, and snow so fine it feels like flour. According to the story, the wind tears the door off its hinges. Then it rips a hole in the roof.
> Minnie has thirteen students... and a choice to make.

**VO 2**
> Stay, and freeze. Or lead them into a storm where you can't see your own hand.
> To understand how these kids ended up in a schoolhouse made of dirt, we have to go back... to a time when almost nobody wanted to live here at all.


### MUSIC M1 fades. SFX S2: dry prairie wind through tall grass → MUSIC M8 (open prairie) in, low

**VO 4: The Great American Desert** *(Q1)*
> In 1820, an army explorer named Stephen Long crossed the Great Plains, and he was not impressed. On his map, he labeled the whole region "the Great American Desert."
> It wasn't really a desert. But it had little rain, almost no trees for lumber or firewood, and no big rivers to ship crops on. The ground was covered in thick sod, with roots so tangled that wooden plows bounced right off. And the Plains were home to powerful Native nations.
> So most settlers just passed through on their way to Oregon or California. And Southern lawmakers blocked plans to give the land away, fearing it would create new states without slavery.
> Then the South left the Union. And the bill finally passed.

### SFX S3: quill pen scratching on paper

**VO 5: The Homestead Act** *(Q2)*
> In 1862, President Lincoln signed the Homestead Act. Any adult citizen, or immigrant planning to become one, including single women and widows, could claim 160 acres of public land. Pay a small fee, build a home, farm the land, and live on it for five years. Then it was yours. Free.
> A Union soldier named Daniel Freeman supposedly talked a land office clerk into opening just after midnight on New Year's Day, 1863, so he could file before heading back to duty. He's often called the very first homesteader.
> In the end, about ten percent of all U.S. land was given away this way.
> Not everyone played fair. Stories say the rules called for a twelve-by-fourteen house, so some cheaters built one that was twelve by fourteen... inches.

### MUSIC M8 out. SFX S4: steamboat whistle on a river, paddlewheel churning → MUSIC M9 (Exodusters) in

**VO 6: Exodusters** *(Q3)*
> [serious] For some settlers, the stakes were even higher.
> After Reconstruction ended in 1877, violence, unfair laws, and sharecropping debt trapped Black families in the South who had only recently escaped slavery.
> A formerly enslaved carpenter named Benjamin "Pap" Singleton had an answer: Kansas. He spread flyers across the South promising land and freedom.
> In 1879, thousands of Black families headed west, many with almost nothing. They called themselves Exodusters, after the Book of Exodus, where the Israelites escape slavery for a promised land.
> Some settled in Nicodemus, Kansas, an all-Black town on the prairie whose first residents lived in homes dug into the ground. Nicodemus is still there today.

### MUSIC M9 out. SFX S5: camera shutter click, old-fashioned flash powder pop → MUSIC M10 (homestead life) in

**VO 7: Sodbusters** *(Q4)*
> So what was homesteading actually like?
> In 1880, a young man named Solomon Butcher tried it in Nebraska and quit almost right away. So he picked up a camera instead, and photographed the families who stayed.
> And what his pictures show is sod.

### SFX S6: plow blade tearing through thick, rooted soil

**VO 8**
> With no trees, settlers built houses out of the ground itself. They cut the prairie sod into bricks, nicknamed "Nebraska marble," and stacked them into walls. A sod house stayed warm in winter and cool in summer. It also dripped mud for days after a rain. And every so often, a snake dropped out of the ceiling.
> That's why Plains farmers were called sodbusters: they had to bust through that tough sod to plant anything, with help from steel plows, windmills, and barbed wire.
> And with no firewood, kids had a chore you probably don't: collecting dried cow and buffalo manure to burn in the stove.

### MUSIC M10 out. SFX S7: massive buzzing insect swarm rising → MUSIC M11 (hardship) in

**VO 9: Hard times** *(Q5)*
> [serious] But the Plains could be brutal.
> Droughts wiped out whole harvests. And in 1874, swarms of grasshoppers so huge they blocked out the sun came down on the Plains. They ate the crops, then the leaves off the trees, then clothes on the line, and even the wooden handles of tools.
> Money was just as dangerous. Farmers borrowed to buy land and machines, but as farms grew more wheat and corn, crop prices fell. Railroads charged high rates to ship the harvest. And banks wanted their loans paid back.
> [sad] Many lost their farms, became tenant farmers working someone else's land, or gave up and went back East. Some painted a message on their wagons as they left: "In God we trusted. In Kansas we busted."

### MUSIC M11 out. SFX S1 returns (blizzard) → MUSIC M7 in

**VO 10: The Children's Blizzard** *(Q6)*
> [serious] And then there was the weather. Back to Minnie Freeman's schoolhouse.
> The blizzard swept across Nebraska and the Dakota Territory. Temperatures crashed to forty below zero. People said the snow froze their eyes shut.
> Minnie led her students to a farmhouse less than a mile away. In some versions of the story, she tied them together with twine so no one would get lost. Every one of them survived.
> [somber] Many others weren't so lucky. Around 235 people died, many of them children walking home from school. It became known as the Children's Blizzard.

### MUSIC M7 out. SFX S8: 1890s fairground crowd, brass band in the distance → MUSIC M12 (World's Fair) in, low

**VO 11: Turner's Frontier Thesis** *(Q7)*
> In 1893, at the Chicago World's Fair, a young historian named Frederick Jackson Turner made a big claim: the frontier was closed.
> He argued the frontier had made Americans independent, self-reliant, and democratic. And it had worked as a "safety valve of discontent." When life in crowded Eastern cities got hard, unhappy people could head west for cheap land, so that pressure never exploded into revolution.
> So if the frontier was gone... where would that pressure go?
> Funny thing is, hardly anyone noticed his speech. Just down the road, Buffalo Bill's Wild West show was drawing huge crowds by acting out a frontier Turner said was already over.

### MUSIC M12 out → MUSIC M1 (theme) in, low

**VO 12: Close**
> [reflective] The Homestead Act promised free land. For some families, that promise came true. For many others, the Plains took everything.
> But there's a question we haven't asked yet. All that land, given away 160 acres at a time... who was living on it before?
> [serious] Next time: the Plains Wars, and the road to Wounded Knee.
