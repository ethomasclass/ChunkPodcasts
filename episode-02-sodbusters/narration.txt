[curious] In 1820, an army explorer named Stephen Long crossed the Great Plains, and he was not impressed. On his map, he labeled the whole region "the Great American Desert."
[interested] It wasn't really a desert. But it had little rain, almost no trees for lumber or firewood, and no big rivers to ship crops on. The ground was covered in thick sod, with roots so tangled that wooden plows bounced right off. And the Plains were home to powerful Native nations.
So most settlers just passed through on their way to Oregon or California. And Southern lawmakers blocked plans to give the land away, fearing it would create new states without slavery.
[matter-of-fact] Then the South left the Union. And the bill finally passed.

--- VO 5 ---
[matter-of-fact] In 1862, President Lincoln signed the Homestead Act. Any adult citizen, or immigrant planning to become one, including single women and widows, could claim 160 acres of public land. Pay a small fee, build a home, farm the land, and live on it for five years. Then it was yours. Free.
[interested] A Union soldier named Daniel Freeman supposedly talked a land office clerk into opening just after midnight on New Year's Day, 1863, so he could file before heading back to duty. He's often called the very first homesteader.
[interested] In the end, about ten percent of all U.S. land was given away this way.
[matter-of-fact] Not everyone played fair. Stories say the rules called for a twelve-by-fourteen house, so some cheaters built one that was twelve by fourteen... inches.

--- VO 6 ---
[serious] For some settlers, the stakes were even higher.
After Reconstruction ended in 1877, violence, unfair laws, and sharecropping debt trapped Black families in the South who had only recently escaped slavery.
A formerly enslaved carpenter named Benjamin "Pap" Singleton had an answer: Kansas. He spread flyers across the South promising land and freedom.
[determined] In 1879, thousands of Black families headed west, many with almost nothing. They called themselves Exodusters, after the Book of Exodus, where the Israelites escape slavery for a promised land.
[interested] Some settled in Nicodemus, Kansas, an all-Black town on the prairie whose first residents lived in homes dug into the ground. Nicodemus is still there today.

--- VO 7 ---
[calm] So what was homesteading actually like?
In 1880, a young man named Solomon Butcher tried it in Nebraska and quit almost right away. So he picked up a camera instead, and photographed the families who stayed.
And what his pictures show is sod.

--- VO 8 ---
[interested] With no trees, settlers built houses out of the ground itself. They cut the prairie sod into bricks, nicknamed "Nebraska marble," and stacked them into walls. A sod house stayed warm in winter and cool in summer. It also dripped mud for days after a rain. And every so often, a snake dropped out of the ceiling.
That's why Plains farmers were called sodbusters: they had to bust through that tough sod to plant anything, with help from steel plows, windmills, and barbed wire.
[matter-of-fact] And with no firewood, kids had a chore you probably don't: collecting dried cow and buffalo manure to burn in the stove.

--- VO 9 ---
[serious] But the Plains could be brutal.
Droughts wiped out whole harvests. And in 1874, swarms of grasshoppers so huge they blocked out the sun came down on the Plains. They ate the crops, then the leaves off the trees, then clothes on the line, and even the wooden handles of tools.
[thoughtful] Money was just as dangerous. Farmers borrowed to buy land and machines, but as farms grew more wheat and corn, crop prices fell. Railroads charged high rates to ship the harvest. And banks wanted their loans paid back.
[sad] Many lost their farms, became tenant farmers working someone else's land, or gave up and went back East. Some painted a message on their wagons as they left: "In God we trusted. In Kansas we busted."

--- VO 10 ---
[serious] And then there was the weather. Back to Minnie Freeman's schoolhouse.
The blizzard swept across Nebraska and the Dakota Territory. Temperatures crashed to forty below zero. People said the snow froze their eyes shut.
[interested] Minnie led her students to a farmhouse less than a mile away. In some versions of the story, she tied them together with twine so no one would get lost. Every one of them survived.
[somber] Many others weren't so lucky. Around 235 people died, many of them children walking home from school. It became known as the Children's Blizzard.

--- VO 11 ---
[interested] In 1893, at the Chicago World's Fair, a young historian named Frederick Jackson Turner made a big claim: the frontier was closed.
[thoughtful] He argued the frontier had made Americans independent, self-reliant, and democratic. And it had worked as a "safety valve of discontent." When life in crowded Eastern cities got hard, unhappy people could head west for cheap land, so that pressure never exploded into revolution.
[curious] So if the frontier was gone... where would that pressure go?
[matter-of-fact] Funny thing is, hardly anyone noticed his speech. Just down the road, Buffalo Bill's Wild West show was drawing huge crowds by acting out a frontier Turner said was already over.
